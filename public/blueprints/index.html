Blueprints, Rev D, 14 October 2026

The shell, the joint and the tests, with dimensions.

The facility is a closed shell 11.15 m across, in the shape of a football. It has 20 glass hexagons, which are the windows, and 12 solid pentagons, which hold the solar panels and the shields that fold out over the windows. Inside is air at 1 atm and outside is vacuum. So the air pushes out on every panel, with about 1.33 MN on each hexagon, and the whole shell is in tension, pulled outward everywhere. It can fail by breaking glass, by leaking at a seam or by pulling a seam apart, but not by buckling. Rev D is the version of our design that we signed on 14 October 2026, and it replaced the earlier Rev S. It is the first design sized by a stress model of the whole shell, and it has 24 glass panes per hexagon on deep ribs, a solid node at every corner and a hardpoint halfway along every seam.

  • 11,151 mm across
  • 2,250 mm edges
  • 20 glass hexagons with 24 panes each
  • 12 solid pentagons
  • 90 seams and 60 corners
  • 2,340 bolts that clamp the seals

The baseline

Rev D, 14 October 2026, the design sized by our stress model

There are 13 sheets at A3 size. Click any sheet to open it full size.

From 9 to 12 October we ran a stress model of the whole shell in three different programs, and it was reviewed independently. It showed that the Rev S design cannot hold 1 atm. In the six-pane hexagon, the 31 mm glass reaches 54 MPa, against the 35 MPa we allow, and it bends 7.3 mm, against a limit of 3.1 mm. The 103 mm ribs go far past yield, the point where the metal bends for good. And the open corners put 100 to 138 kN on the end bolts, which break at 34 kN.

Rev D takes a route the spec already allowed for. Each hexagon is split into 24 triangular panes, 1,125 mm on a side, on main ribs 375 mm deep and secondary ribs 160 mm deep. The pressure pane is 25.4 mm BOROFLOAT 33. Air pressure pulls across each seam with 623 kN. That pull goes through a solid machined block at every corner, the corner node, and a pinned fitting halfway along every seam, the hardpoint. Both are proposed details, sized on 16 October. So the 26 bolts along a seam only clamp the seals. The structure weighs 34.0 t, and the whole facility about 50.4 t.

Every item on the sheets has a status tag. LOCKED means the geometry has not changed since Rev S. FEA means our computer stress models set it in the October runs. That covers the rib sections, the glass thickness, the number of panes and how the load is split. PROPOSAL means a detail drawn to carry a load from the models. It was sized in the models of 16 October and still has to pass a physical test. The proposals are the corner node and its tie bolts, the hardpoint, the count of 26 bolts and the wider seal grooves. OPEN means a decision we have not taken yet, with the Rev D default stated. Rev D listed ten open decisions, and they are at the bottom of this page. Rev D is the signed version of TRUTH, our master spec.

On 23 October we took two of the open decisions for Rev E, the next version of the spec, with drawings due on 8 November. The pressure pane becomes 22 mm BOROFLOAT 33, in 531 mm panes, 96 to a hexagon. The seal becomes two loops of butyl BA500 with the RTV566 bead behind them. Until the new drawings come out, the sheets below show the Rev D pane and seal. The plan page gives the reasons.

How to read the drawings

  • Each Rev D sheet is one A3 drawing. Its title block gives the sheet, the material and heat treatment of the parts on it, and the status of every item as LOCKED, FEA, PROPOSAL or OPEN. The sheets are not to scale, so read the dimensions.
  • A Rev S 2D sketch is a drawing in a hand-drawn style, cut from the model.
  • A Rev S 2D render is a flat, shaded view or a cut through a part.
  • A Rev S 3D sketch and 3D render show the same tilted view, one as a line drawing and one as a render, shown one above the other.
  • In the Rev S drawings, blue lines are dimensions in millimetres, taken from the model. A dimension drawn on a tilted view still gives the true length.

Click any drawing to open it full size. The arrow keys on your keyboard step through them.

How the pieces fit

The shell is made of 32 panels. Each panel has a frame along each edge. Two frames bolted together make a seam, and three seams meet at a corner. The stress model of the whole shell, run from 9 to 12 October, shows how the load is shared. Most of the pull across a seam goes into the corner nodes and about a third into the hardpoint halfway along the seam. The bolts only clamp the seals.

We test the parts that decide whether the shell holds. T1 tests the seam. We build it this fall and pull it apart at the Rev D load in late January and February 2027. T0 tests the glass by bending bars at four points in spring 2027. T2 tests one hexagon panel at 1:4 scale in spring 2027, and T3 tests a corner in 2027. Every test runs on the computer first, and nothing is machined until its digital twin, the computer model of that test, has a signed sheet. A test passes when the measurement comes within 10 percent of the prediction.

Rev D is a design in progress, not a design ready to fly. The open decisions are listed at the bottom of this page.

The structure

The whole shell

There is no physical test of the whole shell this year. A size study with computer stress models (FEA) comes first.

This is the complete pressure shell. Its shape is a truncated icosahedron, like a football, 11,151 mm across the corners, with every edge 2,250 mm long. It has 32 flat panels joined along 90 seams that meet at 60 corners. The 20 glass hexagons are the windows, and the 12 solid pentagons hold the solar panels and the shields that fold out. Every seam has 29 bolts, so the shell has 2,610 of them.

This is the whole structure. Every other section of this page is a piece of it.

Key numbers

  • 1 atm (101.3 kPa) inside, vacuum outside
  • 1.33 MN pushing out on each hexagon
  • Up to 277 kN per metre across each seam, at the upper bound
  • The model as drawn weighs about 26,700 kg. That is not an estimate, because the ribs are still the old ones, which are too weak.

Why it is like this

  • A sphere is the best shape for holding air in, because the pressure pulls on the wall evenly everywhere. This shape is close to a sphere but is built only from flat panels, and flat glass is what glass makers can make at this size.
  • It uses just two panel shapes, so every hexagon is the same part and every pentagon is the same part. That keeps the design modular.
  • The 2,250 mm edge sets the panel size, so a hexagon is 4.5 m from corner to corner.
  • The pentagons are not windows. Each one holds solar panels and a shield that folds out to cover the hexagons around it. Every hexagon borders three pentagons, so the twelve pentagons can reach every window. In the model each pentagon is still only its frames, ribs and a 4 mm plate, because the housing is not designed yet.

How it is tested

  • We do not test the whole shell physically this year.
  • The open question is how the shell passes the load between panels. Depending on the path, the pull across a seam is anywhere from 35 to 277 kN per metre. A study with computer models of one panel, a cap of seven panels and the full shell settles it first.
  • We would test a closed shell at 1:10 scale, 1.12 m across, under water, but only if the study says a cap is not enough. That would be spring 2027 at the earliest.

The hexagon panel

T2 tests one at 1:4 scale, and T0 tests the glass.

This is one glass panel, 4,500 mm from corner to corner, 3,897 mm across the flat sides and 140 mm deep. It has six edge frames around the outside, six ribs running from a 160 mm hub to the corners, and six triangular windows. In this model each window has two panes. L1 is a 6 mm outer pane of fused silica under the outer flange. L7 is the 31 mm borosilicate pressure pane, which sits on the seat lip. The layers between them, the gas gap, the solar and tint glass and the shutters, are left out of this model.

The shell has 20 of these, and they make up most of it.

Key numbers

  • The pane sees about 53 MPa at 1 atm
  • The pane bends 7.2 mm, against a 3.1 mm limit, if the seat acts like a hinge
  • 1.33 MN on the panel at 1 atm

Why it is like this

  • One pane 4.5 m across would be far too thick and heavy. The ribs split the window into six triangles of about 2.2 square metres each.
  • The pressure pane sits on the cabin side of its seat lip, so the cabin air presses it onto the seat instead of pushing it off.
  • The frame is 140 mm deep because it has to hold all the layers of glass.
  • The ribs work like spokes. Nothing holds the hub, so each rib carries its share of the pane load all the way to a corner. That is why our hand calculations say the current rib is far too weak, at about 2,900 MPa at 1 atm. The rib study in week 6 makes the ribs deeper or adds a ring.

How it is tested

  • T2 tests one at 1:4 scale, with aluminium panes in place of glass, to see how the panes bend onto the frame and ribs.
  • T0 measures how strong the glass really is.
  • We would test a full-size panel only with an industrial partner.

The pentagon panel

It is not tested on its own, and T1 covers its seams.

This is one solid panel, not a window. It is 3,641 mm from corner to corner, 3,462 mm from a corner to the opposite flat side, and 140 mm deep. In the design it holds solar panels and a shield that folds out over the five hexagons around it. So far the model has only its structure, which is five edge frames, five ribs to a hub and a 4 mm face plate of 6061-T6 aluminium. The housing, the solar panels and the shields are not drawn yet.

The shell has 12 of these. One is at the top (PENT-00), one is at the bottom, and the rest are spread evenly between the hexagons.

Key numbers

  • About 0.88 MN pushing out on each pentagon at 1 atm

Why it is like this

  • Putting the solar panels and the shields on the pentagons keeps all 20 hexagons clear as windows.
  • Each pentagon sits in the middle of five hexagons, so a shield stowed there can fold out over its neighbours. Every hexagon borders three pentagons.
  • It has the same frames, joint and depth as the hexagons, so every seam in the shell is the same.
  • Its frames are always the grooved B type, so all five frames of every pentagon are one part.
  • Two things are still open. One is where the housing sits, inside the 140 mm depth of the frame or standing out from the shell. The other is what a shield folding out puts into the pentagon frame and the corners.

How it is tested

  • We do not test it on its own this year. Its seams are the same as the hexagon seams, so T1 covers them.
  • The housing, the solar panels and the shield mechanism get their own design and tests later. A shield folding out over five hexagons loads the pentagon frame in ways that T0 to T2 do not cover.

The simple joint, cut open

T1 tests it with seam slices and the 550 mm joint piece.

This is a cut through one seam at a bolt. Two frames sit face to face on the joint plane. Each frame has a 20 mm web, an outer flange of 50 by 10 mm at the outside face, and a seat lip of 50 by 10 mm that holds the pressure pane. There is one 5/16 inch bolt every 75 mm. Frame B has two O-ring grooves and the RTV bead, and Frame A is flat.

This section runs the full length of all 90 seams.

Key numbers

  • Up to 277 kN per metre across the joint at 1 atm, at the upper bound
  • 20.7 kN per bolt, almost three times the 7.4 kN in TRUTH, our master spec
  • Seals at 18, 32 and 42 mm and the bolt at 58 mm from the outside tip

Why it is like this

  • Two flat faces bolted together is the simplest joint that still seals. Nothing slides or keys into anything, so it is easy to machine and to check.
  • There are three seals in a row, two O-rings and a bead of RTV silicone. One can fail without a leak. A small port between the O-rings lets us check each one for leaks on its own.
  • All the grooves are in one frame, B, so the two frames never have to line up groove to groove. A pentagon is always B. Between two hexagons, the one with the lower number is A.
  • The joint face lies on the plane through the seam and the centre of the shell. That way the three joint faces at every corner meet on one line and the corner closes. It is why the flanges sit at an angle, 20.9 degrees on a hexagon, and why the section looks like a Y.
  • We dropped several features of the older Rev C joint: the tongue and groove, the key boss, the cable conduit, the looped groove ends and the guide pins.

How it is tested

  • T1 pulls short slices of it apart and checks the seal while it is loaded.
  • The 550 mm joint piece checks that it goes together and holds pressure.

One seam along its length

T1 tests slices of three sizes, with 1, 2 and 3 bolts.

One full seam is 2,250 mm long, with 29 bolts set 75 mm apart. The end bolts are 75 mm from each corner. The O-ring grooves run the whole length and stop 30 mm short of each corner.

There are 90 of these, one along every edge of the shell.

Key numbers

  • 620 kN across a whole 2,250 mm seam at 1 atm, at the upper bound
  • 29 bolts per seam, 2,610 in the shell

Why it is like this

  • The 75 mm spacing between bolts is set in TRUTH.
  • We use 29 bolts, not 30. With 30, the end nuts of two seams between a hexagon and a pentagon run into each other at every pentagon corner. With 29 there is 75 mm to each corner and nothing touches. TRUTH says about 30, so this needs a decision.

How it is tested

  • The T1 slices have 1, 2 and 3 bolts. If the load and the gap per bolt stay the same as the slice gets longer, the whole seam of 29 bolts behaves like the slices.

The corner, where three panels meet

A corner test, T3, comes later, once the corner seal is designed.

This is the corner where HEX-09, HEX-14 and PENT-10 meet, cut out as a 1.4 m square around the corner point. The panes are left out so the frames show.

There are 60 corners in the shell, all like this one, with two hexagons and a pentagon.

Key numbers

  • 100 mm across the two outer flanges of a seam
  • 75 mm from the corner to the first bolt

Why it is like this

  • Because every joint face lies on a plane through the centre of the shell, the three seams meet on one line through the corner and close with no gap.
  • The ribs stop short of the corner and have pockets cut round the end bolts, so nothing clashes.
  • The corner seal is still open, as item O10. The O-rings of the three seams stop 30 mm from the corner, and nothing yet seals the small triangle where they meet.

How it is tested

  • A corner test, T3, comes after T1 and T2 agree with the model and after the corner seal is designed.
  • If the load in the plane of the panels runs along the seams, each corner takes about 621 kN, which could make the corners the most loaded places in the shell.

The tests

T0: glass discs pressed between two rings

This is the test. We press 30 or more discs of each glass between two rings, following the standard ASTM C1499.

A glass disc, 50 mm across and 3 mm thick, sits on a 40 mm support ring. A 20 mm load ring presses on it from above, as ASTM C1499 sets out. The fixture is 70 mm across.

It tests the material of the glass panes, not a part of the structure.

Key numbers

  • 50 mm disc, 3 mm thick
  • 40 mm support ring, 20 mm load ring
  • 30 or more discs of each glass we consider

Why it is like this

  • The stress in the pane, about 53 MPa at 1 atm, is the number we trust least. Glass breaks at its worst flaw, so its strength scatters and drops as the piece gets bigger.
  • Pressing between two rings loads the middle of the disc evenly in every direction, like a pane bending under pressure. It keeps the load away from the edges, so the edge finish does not decide the result.
  • With 30 or more discs of each glass we get a Weibull strength, a statistical measure set out in ASTM C1239, that can be scaled up to a pane of 2 square metres. For example, discs at 150 MPa with a Weibull modulus of 7 mean about 42 MPa for a full pane, before any safety factor.
  • One set, loaded at four speeds, measures slow crack growth as ASTM C1368 sets out. Glass under a steady load gets weaker over time.

How it is tested

  • It runs in a small 5 kN load frame in October.
  • A load of 0.9 kN puts 50 MPa in the middle of a disc, and the loads run up to about 1 to 4 kN.

T1: pulling a slice of the seam apart

This is the test. We pull the slice apart in a 100 kN frame and check it for leaks while it is loaded.

This is a full-size slice of one seam, 75, 150 or 225 mm long, with 1, 2 or 3 bolts. The outer flange and the seat lip run out to 200 mm as legs to grip, and a steel pull block bolts between them. End dams close the O-ring grooves at the cut ends. The piece is 410 mm long overall.

It is a short piece of the seam, cut from anywhere along its length.

Key numbers

  • The 150 mm slice takes 41.5 kN at 1 atm and runs to 2 atm, 83 kN
  • The 75 mm slice runs to 2.4 atm and the 225 mm slice to 1.5 atm

Why it is like this

  • Bolts, O-rings and grooves do not scale down, so the seam is tested at full size.
  • A whole seam at 1 atm pulls about 620 kN, far beyond a lab frame. A 150 mm slice pulls 41.5 kN, which a 100 kN frame can do.
  • We test three lengths on Tengxiao Li's advice: try a few sizes and see when the response per bolt stops changing.
  • The pull goes in through the flange and lip, the same parts that carry the panel's load in the shell.
  • The grooves run out of the cut ends. End plates faced with rubber and clamped by M8 rods close them, so the space between the O-rings can hold pressure.

How it is tested

  • In run 1 we pull through both legs, midway between the flange and the lip.
  • In run 2 we remove the lip bolts and pull through the flange only, so the joint pries open.
  • We measure the load and the stroke, the gap at the outside and cabin faces, the strain in one bolt, and the pressure between the O-rings.
  • It passes if the response is a straight line up to the 1 atm load and returns to zero, with no leak at 1 atm. The gap at the inner O-ring must stay under 0.33 mm, a provisional limit. Nothing may yield below proof pressure, and the model must agree within 10 percent.

T1: the 550 mm joint piece

This is the test. We fit it together dry, then test for leaks between the O-rings.

This is 550 mm of the joint with no legs to grip. It has both frames, 7 bolts set 75 mm apart, both O-rings, the RTV bead and end dams. It is 100 mm across the flanges and 140 mm deep.

It is a longer piece of the same seam.

Key numbers

  • 7 bolts
  • It weighs 11 kg

Why it is like this

  • To pull it at the full seam load would take 152 to 365 kN, too much for a 100 kN frame. So we use it to check fit and seal, not strength.
  • It is long enough to show whether a straight run of the joint goes together and seals the way the drawings say.

How it is tested

  • First comes a dry fit. We assemble it, tighten the bolts and check how much the O-rings are squeezed.
  • Then comes a leak test, load case LC5. We put 1 atm of air between the two O-rings and watch how fast the pressure drops.

T2: one panel at 1:4 scale

This is the test piece. We load it with water in the T2 rig.

This is a hexagon panel at quarter scale, 1,125 mm from corner to corner, 974 mm across the flat sides and 124 mm deep. Its frame webs are 12 mm thick, with a support flange of 40 by 10 mm for the rig. It has six T-shaped ribs 110 mm deep and six 8 mm aluminium panes on neoprene setting strips, held in by beads of 3 by 10 mm. It weighs 47 kg.

It is a model of one hexagon panel.

Key numbers

  • 83 kN at 1 atm, 167 kN at 2 atm
  • The pane sees 53 MPa at 1 atm, the same as at full size

Why it is like this

  • If every length is divided by 4, the stresses stay the same at the same pressure, and a 1.1 m panel fits in a shop. The force at 1 atm is 83 kN instead of 1.33 MN.
  • At this scale, aluminium panes bend almost like the 31 mm glass would, and 7.4 mm panes would match exactly. They do not shatter, and they can go past 1 atm.
  • The ribs are new. We sized them by hand as spokes, so they stay elastic up to 2 atm, with 91 MPa at 1 atm and 183 MPa at 2 atm.
  • Some things do not scale cleanly, such as the setting strips, the beads and the web widths. That is one of our questions for Prof. Henann.

How it is tested

  • We load it with water in the T2 rig, shown in the next section.
  • Strain rosettes go on both faces of a pane centre and on the opposite pane, on the rib flanges halfway along, and on the frame web at mid-edge and near a corner. We also measure how far the hub and a pane centre move.

T2: the hexagonal water rig

This is the test rig. We run it with the panel edge pinned and with it clamped, up to 2 atm.

This is a hexagonal steel tub with 12 mm walls, a 25 mm floor and a depth of 175 mm, with a 25 mm box flange. The panel sits cabin side down on a 1 mm rubber membrane. The membrane has its own clamp ring, and a hexagonal top ring is bolted down with 24 M16 bolts. The rig is 1,409 by 1,220 mm and 350 mm tall, with about 490 kg of steel and 165 L of water.

This is test equipment, not part of the facility.

Key numbers

  • 700 kg when full
  • 24 M16 bolts, 3.5 kN each at 1 atm
  • The relief valve opens at 220 kPa

Why it is like this

  • Water hardly compresses, so if something breaks almost no stored energy is released. Air at the same pressure would store far more.
  • The rings are hexagonal so they follow the panel all the way round.
  • The membrane carries the water pressure onto the panes and keeps the water in. It does no structural work.
  • The membrane has its own clamp ring so its seal holds even when the panel lifts.
  • We test two ways of holding the edge. When it is pinned, the panel's support flange rests on 12 mm round bars, so the edge can turn like a hinge. When it is clamped, the top ring presses straight on the flange, so the edge cannot turn. The real edge in the shell is somewhere between, and the pane only meets its bending limit if the edge acts close to clamped.

How it is tested

  • We write down the model's prediction for every gauge before any water goes in.
  • We fill the rig, bleed out all the air and check that the relief valve opens at 220 kPa.
  • We test the pinned edge first. We go from 0 to 1 atm in steps of 0.25 atm, hold each step for 2 minutes and unload the same way, three times. Then we do the same with the edge clamped.
  • Only then do we go to 1.5 and 2 atm, in steps of 0.1 atm above 1 atm. The dial gauges read against a bridge clamped to the panel frame, so any movement of the rig drops out.

Still open

Rev D left ten decisions open, each with a default. We took two of them on 23 October for Rev E, the pressure glass and the seal, so eight are still open.

  • The hardpoint halfway along the seam can be pinned, which is the Rev D default, or stiff enough to carry bending. That choice sets the secondary rib at 160 or 200 mm deep. Submodel C3, a detailed computer model of the hardpoint, covered it on 16 October.
  • We decided the pressure glass on 23 October, for Rev E. It is 22 mm BOROFLOAT 33 in 531 mm panes, 16 to each 2,250 mm triangle and 96 to a hexagon. If the glass proves very consistent we can go up to 708 mm panes, and if it scatters a lot we go down to 425 mm. We make the final pick after the T0 bars give us the Weibull modulus m, the number that says how much the strength of the glass varies. The drawings come out on 8 November. Until then the Rev D sheets above show 25.4 mm glass in 1,125 mm panes.
  • We decided the seal on 23 October, for Rev E. It is two closed loops of butyl BA500, with the RTV566 bead as a third seal. On Parker's advice we dropped the fluorosilicone cord. The drawings come out on 8 November.
  • The part number of the seam bolt is still open. The old number, NAS9306C-06, is a rivet standard and the wrong size. The parts memo of 1 November names the right bolt.
  • How much the frame bows between corners is still open. The models predict 1.4 to 2.0 mm, against a limit of 1 mm for the seals to work. The default is to keep the frame 140 mm deep and test it, and the other choice is to make it deeper. We decide after T1 and submodel C2.
  • The corner seal is still open. It sits in the plug at the corner point, where the three seal loops meet.
  • The tie bolts at each corner and the 40 mm hardpoint pin are still open. The ties carry tension only, and the bending at the corner goes through the faces of the blocks. Submodels C1 and C3 covered them on 16 October.
  • The mass is still open. In Rev D the structure weighs 34.0 t and the facility 50.4 t, up from a Rev S figure that did not hold. The next step to bring it down is a rib study aimed at clearer views, with narrower and deeper ribs.
  • Rev D has no shear key, the part that stops the two frames of a seam sliding along each other. The dowels at the corners and the hardpoint pin carry that sliding load instead. We bring the shear key back only if submodel C2 or the shear check on the whole shell needs it.
  • The safety factor is still open. So far we have used 1.5 on yield at 1 atm. The other choice is the NASA-STD-5001 rule, with 1.5 at proof and 2.0 at ultimate failure. We agree this with our faculty advisors before the 8 November drawings.
