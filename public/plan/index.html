The plan, fall 2026. Written 28 September 2026, Rev D note added 14 October 2026, updated 23 October 2026

This fall we build the seam between two panels. We pull it apart in January.

We take the joint where two panels meet from a design on paper to hardware we have built and measured, and we do the analysis it depends on (loads, glass strength, frame material) so the tests mean something. Every test runs on the computer first.

23 Oct 2026: what the October meetings changed

Two design decisions, labelled Rev E, decided 23 October, drawings 8 November. The pressure pane becomes 22 mm BOROFLOAT 33 on 531 mm panes: each 2,250 mm triangle is split into 16 panes, 96 per hexagon and 1,920 on the shell, because the glass makers can finish a square no larger than 787 mm and a smaller pane makes the glass thinner and safer. 708 mm panes are the upside and 425 mm the fallback; the pick is final once T0 measures how much the glass strength scatters. The seal becomes two butyl BA500 loops with the RTV566 bead kept as the third seal, in place of fluorosilicone cord, on Parker's advice. The blueprints and the 3D model stay Rev D until the 8 November pack. Cabin pressure stays 1 atm. Three things in the test plan change. The glass test moves from discs to bars in four-point bend, 1 in by 1/8 in, the way ENGN 1440 runs it, in spring 2027: the disc fixture would cost thousands, and the small frame at Brown is 0.5 kN, not the 5 kN the plan assumed. The seam pulls move to late January to February 2027 on the 100 kN frame at Brown, which is free after 18 November; Dewesoft lends the 16-channel strain recorder in 3 to 4 week batches with a month's notice. This fall is fabrication. And one rule: every physical test is completed on the computer first, and nothing is machined before its digital twin has a signed sheet.

14 Oct 2026: Rev D

The whole-shell stress model (9 to 12 October, three solvers) showed the Rev S design does not hold 1 atm, and Rev D replaces it: 24 glass panes per hexagon on 375 mm ribs, 25.4 mm glass, a solid node at every corner and a hardpoint at every mid-seam, with the bolts as seal clamps. Three things in this plan change. T0 moves from glass discs to borosilicate bars in four-point bend, run in ENGN 1440 in spring 2027. The T2 article becomes the 24-pane hexagon at 1:4. T1 tests the Rev D seam at the service load of 12 kN per bolt and at the upper bound. The 1:1 seam slices and the 550 mm article go ahead this fall as planned, to the Rev D section. The concept

A room in orbit made mostly of windows.

HCSA is a design for a facility in low Earth orbit for short visits. Nobody lives there; people come to look at Earth, move around, meet, do research and spend time among plants.

Its wall is 32 flat panels in the shape of a football. The 20 hexagons are glass: they are the windows. The 12 pentagons are solid: they house the solar panels and the shields that fold out over the windows around them. Every window borders three pentagons, so a shield can reach every one.

Inside is air at normal pressure and outside is vacuum, so the air pushes out on every panel with about 1.33 meganewtons on each hexagon. Holding that at the seams, and through the glass, is what this fall's work is about.

  • 11.15 m across
  • 2.25 m edges
  • 630 m³ inside
  • 32 panels
  • 90 seams
  • 1 atm inside, vacuum outside

Where we are

The design is simple enough to build.

  • The panel joint is redesigned to be simple (Rev S): two aluminium frames bolted face to face, one row of bolts every 75 mm, two O-rings and a sealant bead.
  • The whole shell is modelled in CAD: 32 panels, 90 seams, 2,610 bolts and every seal, with no parts overlapping.
  • The tests are designed, with drawings for every test piece and rig, and the test plan is going to Brown engineering faculty for review.
  • Every drawing, with dimensions and a plain explanation, is on the blueprints page.
  • Every test as a 23-minute film, with how each piece is made, what the test tells us, plan B, cost and dates, is on the tests page.

The tests, smallest first

Each test answers one question.

T0: how strong is our glass?

Borosilicate glass bars, 1 in wide and 1/8 in thick, bent in four-point bending until they break, the way ENGN 1440 does it. 30 or more per edge finish, so we know the real strength and how much it scatters. That scatter picks the final pane size: 531 mm as planned, 708 mm if the glass is consistent, 425 mm if it is not. The disc test drawn here is off: the fixture would cost thousands and the small frame at Brown is 0.5 kN, not 5 kN. The 60 BOROFLOAT and 15 sapphire discs already received stay for a second look.

Spring 2027, with Prof. Padture's class. Bars this fall on the 0.5 kN frame are still being weighed.

Drawings and dimensions

T1: does the seam hold when it is pulled apart?

Full-size slices of the seam, 75, 150 and 225 mm long, pulled in the 100 kN load frame at Brown while we check the seal between the two butyl loops. Plus a 550 mm piece of the seam to check it goes together and holds air. The seal changed on 23 Oct: two butyl BA500 loops and the silicone bead, in place of fluorosilicone cord, because a closed loop has no cut ends to leak at the corners (Parker's advice).

Built this fall from the 8 November drawings. Pulled late January to February 2027: the frame is free after 18 November, and Dewesoft lends the 16-channel strain recorder in 3 to 4 week batches, booked a month ahead.

Drawings and dimensions

T2: how does a window panel bend?

A hexagon panel at quarter scale, 1.1 m across, with aluminium in place of glass, loaded with water up to 2 atm in a steel tub. It shows how the panes bend onto the frame and ribs.

Spring 2027.

Drawings and dimensions

FEA: how does the whole shell share the load?

Computer models of one panel, a cap of seven panels and the full shell. They tell us how much load the seams and corners really carry, which sets the bolt size.

Ran 9 to 12 October (Rev D). Every physical test now gets its own computer twin first, and nothing is machined before that twin has a signed sheet.
