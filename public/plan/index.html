What the October meetings changed (23 October 2026)

On 23 October we made two design decisions. Both go into Rev E, the next version of our spec, and its drawings come out on 8 November.

The first is the glass. The pressure pane becomes 22 mm BOROFLOAT 33 in 531 mm panes. Each 2,250 mm triangle is split into 16 panes, so there are 96 per hexagon and 1,920 on the shell. We made this change because the glass makers can only finish thick glass in small sheets, and the largest polished square they can make is 787 mm. A smaller pane also carries less stress at the same thickness. If the glass is very consistent we can go up to 708 mm panes, and if it is not we go down to 425 mm. The choice is final once T0, our glass strength test, measures how much the strength of the glass scatters.

The second is the seal. On Parker's advice we replace the fluorosilicone cord with two loops of butyl BA500, and we keep the RTV566 bead as a third seal. The blueprints and the 3D model show Rev D until the 8 November pack. The cabin stays at 1 atm.

Three things in the test plan change. The glass test moves from discs to bars bent at four points, 1 inch by 1/8 inch, the way the ENGN 1440 class runs it, in spring 2027. The disc fixture would cost thousands, and the small frame at Brown only goes to 0.5 kN, not the 5 kN the plan assumed. The seam pulls move to late January and February 2027, on the 100 kN frame at Brown, which is free after 18 November. Dewesoft lends the 16-channel strain recorder in batches of 3 to 4 weeks, with a month's notice. This fall is for fabrication. We also follow one rule. Every physical test is completed on the computer first, and nothing is machined until its digital twin, the computer model of that test, has a signed sheet.

What the glass makers can supply, for the pane sizes we are studying

  • SUPREMAX 33 comes rolled at 34.9 mm, in sheets of 1,000 by 1,500 mm. Ground and polished, it is 29.4 mm thick, and the largest piece Swift Glass can finish is 787 by 787 mm. (Tina Gallo at SCHOTT, 20 October, and Charlie Burke at Swift Glass, 14 and 20 October.)
  • BOROFLOAT 33 comes at 25.4 mm in sheets of 1,150 by 850 mm, at 21 mm in sheets of 1,700 by 1,300 mm, and at 15 mm in sheets of 2,300 by 1,700 mm. (SCHOTT, 20 October.)

Rev D (14 October 2026)

From 9 to 12 October we ran a stress model of the whole shell in three different programs. It showed that the Rev S design does not hold 1 atm, and Rev D replaces it. Rev D has 24 glass panes per hexagon on 375 mm ribs and 25.4 mm glass. It has a solid block at every corner, the corner node, and a pinned fitting halfway along every seam, the hardpoint. The bolts only clamp the seals.

Three things in this plan change. T0, the glass test, moves from glass discs to borosilicate bars bent at four points, in the ENGN 1440 class in spring 2027. The test piece for T2, the panel test, becomes the hexagon with 24 panes, at 1:4 scale. T1, the seam test, tests the Rev D seam at the normal working load of 12 kN per bolt and at the upper bound. The full-size seam slices and the 550 mm piece go ahead this fall as planned, cut to the Rev D section. The Rev D pack is on the blueprints page.

The concept

A room in orbit made mostly of windows.

HCSA is a design for a facility in low Earth orbit, for short visits. Nobody lives there. People come to look at Earth, move around, meet, do research and spend time among plants.

Its wall is made of 32 flat panels, in the shape of a football. The 20 hexagons are glass, and they are the windows. The 12 pentagons are solid, and they hold the solar panels and the shields that fold out over the windows around them. Every window borders three pentagons, so a shield can reach every one.

Inside is air at normal pressure and outside is vacuum, so the air pushes out on every panel, with about 1.33 meganewtons on each hexagon. This fall's work is about holding that push at the seams and through the glass.

  • 11.15 m across
  • 2.25 m edges
  • 630 m³ inside
  • 32 panels
  • 90 seams
  • 1 atm inside, vacuum outside

Where we are

The design is simple enough to build.

  • In Rev S we redesigned the panel joint to be simple. Two aluminium frames are bolted face to face, with one row of bolts every 75 mm, two O-rings and a bead of sealant.
  • We modelled the whole shell in CAD, with 32 panels, 90 seams, 2,610 bolts and every seal, and no parts overlap.
  • We have designed the tests and drawn every test piece and rig. The test plan is going to Brown engineering faculty for review.
  • Every drawing, with dimensions and a plain explanation, is on the blueprints page.
  • The tests page has a 23-minute film of every test. It shows how each piece is made, what the test tells us, our plan B, the cost and the dates.

The tests, smallest first

Each test answers one question.

T0: how strong is our glass?

We bend borosilicate glass bars, 1 inch wide and 1/8 inch thick, at four points until they break, the way the ENGN 1440 class does it. We break 30 or more for each edge finish, so we learn the real strength and how much it scatters. That scatter picks the final pane size. We plan on 531 mm, can go to 708 mm if the glass is consistent, and drop to 425 mm if it is not. We have dropped the disc test drawn here. Its fixture would cost thousands, and the small frame at Brown only goes to 0.5 kN, not 5 kN. We keep the 60 BOROFLOAT and 15 sapphire discs we already received for a second look.

This happens in spring 2027, in ENGN 1440, the Brown materials lab course. We are still deciding whether to break a first set of bars this fall on the 0.5 kN frame.

Drawings and dimensions

T1: does the seam hold when it is pulled apart?

We pull full-size slices of the seam, 75, 150 and 225 mm long, in the 100 kN load frame at Brown, and check the seal between the two butyl loops while we pull. A 550 mm piece of the seam checks that it goes together and holds air. On 23 October we changed the seal to two butyl BA500 loops and the silicone bead, in place of fluorosilicone cord. Parker advised this, because a closed loop has no cut ends that could leak at the corners.

We build it this fall from the 8 November drawings and pull it in late January and February 2027. The frame is free after 18 November, and Dewesoft lends the 16-channel strain recorder in batches of 3 to 4 weeks, booked a month ahead.

Drawings and dimensions

T2: how does a window panel bend?

We load a hexagon panel at quarter scale, 1.1 m across, with water in a steel tub, up to 2 atm. It has aluminium panes in place of glass. The test shows how the panes bend onto the frame and ribs. We redraw this test piece for the Rev E pane before 8 November.

This happens in spring 2027.

Drawings and dimensions

FEA: how does the whole shell share the load?

FEA means our computer stress models. We model one panel, a cap of seven panels and the full shell. They tell us how much load the seams and corners really carry, and that sets the bolt size.

These models ran from 9 to 12 October and set Rev D. Every physical test now gets its own computer twin first, and nothing is machined before that twin has a signed sheet.
