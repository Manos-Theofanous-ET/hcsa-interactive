Human-Centric Space Architecture

A room made of glass, 400 km above the Earth.

HCSA is a design and engineering study for a mostly see-through observation station in low Earth orbit. About six visitors at a time spend one orbit inside: ninety minutes of sunrise, Earth, night and stars, seen through twenty glass walls.

Inside

A tour of the interior.

Six small pods ring the central mast, each with a seat for one or two people and the window in front of it. Plants grow in six greenhouse pentagons and up the mast. Handrails with soft light strips lead from the hatch to the mast, the pods, the greenhouses and the window perches. There are grab handles all the way up the mast, drink pouches with valved straws, and tasting trays of herbs and microgreens. Everything is designed around the people inside.

About the project

What we are designing

The station is a truncated icosahedron, the shape of a football, 11.15 metres across and made of 32 flat panels. The twenty six-sided panels are windows. Each one is a grid of 96 glass panes held in an aluminium frame. The twelve five-sided panels are solid: they carry solar panels and shields that unroll over the windows to protect them.

Inside there is no floor and no up or down. A central mast carries power, water and plants. Six small pods ring it, each a seat for one or two with a window in front, and six greenhouse pentagons grow greens that visitors can taste. Handrails, grab handles and foot loops make it easy to move about.

We are students from Brown University, the Rhode Island School of Design, the Moraitis School in Athens, where Marina Kouroumali leads our group, and the University of Patras, advised by Prof. Rick Fleeter. The work is a feasibility study: we are finding out, with calculations and real tests, whether a glass room like this can hold air in space.

Where we are

The state of the project

Updated 5 November 2026.

Design Decided

The windows are 21 mm panes of BOROFLOAT 33 glass, 96 to each hexagon, sealed with two loops of butyl and a bead of silicone, on thinner aluminium ribs that leave about 61 percent of each window as open glass. On 4 November we chose to run the cabin at 0.8 atm with a little more oxygen, which takes about a fifth of the load off the structure, and to design the station as a module for a commercial space station. The drawings are being finished now.

Computer tests Running

We run stress models of the seam between two panels, the corners where panels meet, and the whole pressurised shell. They show where it would give way first, so the real tests aim at the right places. On the November design the corners and the seams pass at the 0.8 atm cabin, with room to spare.

Test parts Quoting

The seam test pieces are drawn, and we are asking machine shops for prices. The glass for the strength tests has been donated, and quotes for the glass bars are in.

Physical tests Jan – Feb 2027

We pull the seam slices apart on a load frame at Brown, with a strain recorder on loan from Dewesoft. In the spring we break glass bars to measure how strong the glass really is.

Research and talks

What we presented, and how it has moved on

We gave the student keynote at Brown Space Horizons 2026, with a talk, posters, models and a VR walk-through. Since then the engineering has changed a lot. Twenty-nine ideas from our talks, each set beside where it stands today: what still holds, what we improved and what we left behind.
